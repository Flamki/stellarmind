## Findings

Permissive licences such as MIT, BSD and Apache-2.0 let a team embed a dependency in a paid
product and keep its own source closed. Their obligation is mainly attribution, which is cheap
to satisfy and easy to audit. Apache-2.0 additionally grants an explicit patent licence, which
matters when a dependency comes from a company rather than an individual.

Copyleft licences such as GPL, LGPL and AGPL impose conditions on distribution. GPL requires
the whole derived work to carry the same licence, LGPL keeps that requirement at the library
boundary, and AGPL extends it to users who interact with the software over a network. MPL sits
between the two camps: file-level copyleft with permissive combination allowed.

The practical differences for a small team are review cost and the shape of the obligations.
A permissive dependency adds a licence file and a line in a notice list. A copyleft dependency
adds a decision: does the product link against it, ship it, or only call it as a service? That
decision has to be revisited on every upgrade, and it is the upgrade path where teams usually
lose time.

## Recommendation

Standardise on permissive licences (MIT or Apache-2.0) for direct dependencies and require a
short written justification before any copyleft dependency enters the tree. Where a copyleft
library is a genuine fit, prefer the AGPL case only when the product is itself a service and
the team is comfortable publishing its modifications. Record the decision in the dependency
review note so the next person does not re-litigate it.
